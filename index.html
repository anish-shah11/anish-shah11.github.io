About

I'm a PhD student in Computer Engineering at Indiana University Bloomington's Luddy School, where my research centers on sparse computation. What pulls me toward it is a gap I keep running into: modern hardware is extraordinarily good at dense, regular arithmetic and noticeably worse at everything else. The moment structure turns irregular — the moment most of your values are zeros — the bottleneck stops being arithmetic and becomes data movement, and a large share of the machine ends up waiting rather than working. That view came from measurement more than from theory. Benchmarking matrix kernels across CPU and GPU implementations on BigRed200, I watched dense matrix multiply climb to roughly 33 TFLOPS on an A100 while matrix-vector multiply flattened out near 350 GFLOPS — not because the kernel was written badly, but because it had run out of arithmetic to hide memory latency behind. The same lesson turned up in cache behavior, where reordering loops alone shifted L1 miss counts by more than an order of magnitude. Sparse workloads live permanently in that memory-bound regime, which is exactly what makes them interesting to me. Most of my time has gone into inference on specialized hardware. I built a local-to-cloud LLM cascade on an AMD Ryzen AI NPU, bringing up the XRT/XDNA runtime stack and measuring what small models genuinely cost to serve on-device — time-to-first-token, queueing behavior under concurrency, and the point at which a request is better escalated to the cloud than answered locally. Running alongside that, I've been characterizing cold- and warm-start latency for serverless inference functions on the same machine, since a model that responds in milliseconds matters little if the platform needs seconds to hand it a container. Both keep circling the question that drives the sparse work: where does the time actually go, and how much of the hardware is doing something useful? Earlier, before coming to Indiana, I worked closer to the metal — writing simulators for out-of-order superscalar pipelines and configurable cache hierarchies, and building low-level systems code in C++. I like computation as a subject in its own right: the arithmetic, the memory it stirs up, and the distance between what a machine can do on paper and what it manages in practice. Narrowing that distance for workloads that don't fit the dense, regular shape hardware was designed around is what I want to spend my time on.
